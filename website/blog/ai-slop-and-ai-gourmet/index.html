AI slop is real. So is AI gourmet.

17 October 2026 · 10 min read

Some people have called octomon "AI slop". Yes, AI has helped me build it. No, that is not what slop means. Here is what it does mean, and the name I'd like for the other thing.

Since I started posting about octomon, one comment keeps turning up. It arrives in a few different flavours, but it always says the same thing:

AI slop.

Sometimes it comes with a reason. Mostly it doesn't. Sometimes the comment has come from someone who's actually tried the tool, but mostly I don't think they have. So let me address it head on, because I think the people saying it are half right, and the half they have wrong is worth a proper argument.

The food processor #

Let's get the easy part out of the way. If your objection is simply that AI was involved in writing the code, you are the cook who refuses to own a food processor because real chefs chop by hand. Today every professional kitchen has one (and a sous vide or two), and nobody can taste the difference in the soup. Every company that ships software today is using AI to write some of it. Show me one that isn't.

I'll wait.

This isn't a prediction, it's the reality of development right now. The question of whether software should be written with AI was settled while people were still arguing about it. What is left to argue about is whether a given piece of software is any good, and that question has never cared about the tools used to write it. Someone at work mentioned a quote, I can't remember who it's from, but it's accurate.

The difference between AI slop and something better, is you.

Another quote that I have LIVED by my entire career... quotable to no-one but the industry itself.

Shit in, shit out

Finally, while I'm pulling out my favourite quotes, here's another one that has lived with me since working for my first startup in the 90s.

Keep It Simple Stupid (K.I.S.S.)

Which actually applies a LOT to some of the AI coding i've done, because AI often can make a simple task overly complex and that's where the human has to rein it in.

So when "AI slop" means "AI touched it", I'm not interested. When it means something more specific, I am very interested, because slop is real and there is a lot of it.

What I think slop actually is #

Here is what I think people mean when the word is used with accuracy. Someone with a shallow understanding of software asks AI for a thing, gets a thing, and ships the thing. The first version is typically the only version.

  • Nobody asked what happens when the input is hostile.
  • Nobody asked what the program writes to disk, who can read it, or what it sends over the network.
  • Nobody asked what happens on the second machine, or the hundredth hour of uptime, or when the user does the thing nobody expected.
  • Nobody designed the interface, it just fell out of whatever the model produced first.
  • And nobody who was an expert ever looked at it and said "no, that's wrong".

Security, privacy, reliability, change control, user experience. The AI didn't skip those, the person didn't think about them.

As the quote above states, and more than one mentor has told me over the years: shit in, shit out. A model given a vague prompt by someone who can't tell a good answer from a plausible one will produce a plausible one. That is slop. On that, the commenters and I agree.

But let's be honest about something else. By that definition, slop is not new, and it is definitely not an AI invention. A great deal of the software published over the last 30 years was slop. I have used it, I have debugged it, and I have written some of it! Code copied from a forum post without understanding it, then a tutorial, then Stack Overflow, with the variable names changed and the error handling left as an exercise. No threat model, no thought about what it wrote to disk, a UI that was whatever the framework's default looked like, version 1.0 and never a 1.1. The tooling got better and the documentation got better, and the slop got a bit better with it, but it never went away. If anything, a modern model given a lazy prompt often produces something tidier than what a lot of people were shipping in 2005.

So the honest version of the complaint is not that AI produces slop. People have always produced slop and while a lot of it never gained much traction, unfortunately, I am old enough to remember using Internet Explorer 6, Adobe Flash and Lotus Notes. But I digress.

The issue now is that AI produces slop at a volume we have never seen before, because the cost of making it has dropped to nearly nothing. That is a real problem, and the Internet is now full of it. It is also a very different complaint from "this particular tool is slop", and the two keep getting confused.

The other thing needs a name #

But the same tools, in different hands, produce something else entirely. AI is a fantastic way to write very good code, fast. The catch is that everything upstream of the code has to be good first: the idea, the design, the architecture, and yes, the prompts. Give it a clear specification from someone who knows the field and it is like having a tireless pair programmer who has read every RFC and never gets bored of the fiddly bits.

We have a word for the bad end of that spectrum and none for the good end, which is how "AI slop" ends up thrown at everything. So I'd like to coin one. If AI slop is the unappetising, watery, badly prepared meal that looks and tastes like animal feed, then AI gourmet is the Michelin star dish: high quality, a complex flavour profile, specialist preparation, sometimes a rare ingredient or two.

Same kitchen. Same knives. Completely different chef.

What makes it gourmet #

So what are the qualities? Having spent the last two and a half months building octomon this way, I think it comes down to four things.

Iteration, and a lot more of it #

AI lets you prototype, test and throw away in minutes instead of days. The slop mistake is to treat that as a reason to iterate less: the first version arrived so quickly, why not ship it? The gourmet move is the opposite. Faster iteration means you can afford far more of it.

octomon has had 45 releases since the first changelog entry on 11 August. That is not because I'm bad at planning. It's because every release went onto real networks (home fibre, plane Wi-Fi, an Azure VM, a Windows laptop behind NordVPN and WARP) and every one of those networks taught me something the last version got wrong. The changelog is the record. Read a few entries and you'll see the same features being reworked three and four times as the edge cases turned up.

An experienced human in the review loop #

I love that AI can bring software development to people who could never have written a line of Rust before. I genuinely do. But I also believe that right now, to produce production quality software, you need a human in the review process who understands the domain, and that this is the single biggest thing separating gourmet from slop.

While building octomon there were plenty of moments where the AI made an assumption that looked perfectly reasonable and was wrong, and it was 25 years of networking and development experience that let me catch it. Three that come to mind:

  • Targets you add were voting on whether the Internet was up. The first implementation treated every monitored host equally. Then my son's three Fortnite servers, which never answer ICMP, outvoted the built-in targets into "Internet unreachable" while the game played fine. Only the built-in targets should ever decide that. Obvious once you've seen it, not obvious from the code.
  • A gateway that drops pings is not an outage. Airport, hotel and office routers rate-limit or refuse ICMP all the time, and the hops past them are perfectly clean. An early version flagged this as a problem. A network admin knows it's a policy.
  • Switching a VPN on looked like 30% packet loss. During the initial design and build, I never gave VPNs a thought and then while testing I switched mine on. The probe windows reset during the switchover and the first few seconds of loss were a combination of octomon finding its feet and the VPN handover finalizing, but I hadn't designed for this and instead octomon was reporting the connection failing. That needed a settling grace, and it needed someone who knew what a VPN handover actually does on the wire to recognise the pattern.

None of those are things the model got "wrong" in the sense of a bug. It did exactly what was asked (or rather wasn't asked). They are things that only a person who has sat in those networks would know to ask for. That is the review loop, and it isn't optional.

You have to actually understand the code #

Closely related, and I think just as important: you have to be able to read what comes back. Not every line, not every time, but well enough to know when something is wrong, and well enough to write some of it yourself.

In my case I am well versed in Java, Python and the web languages (HTML, CSS, JavaScript), with some C in the distant past. I used octomon as my excuse to learn Rust. So the language was new to me, but none of the concepts were. A loop is a loop, a struct is a struct, and a function that returns an error you then ignore is a bug in any language. The borrow checker was new. The idea that you should think about who owns a piece of memory was not.

That is a very different position from not understanding the code at all. When the model produced something that compiled and looked plausible, I could tell whether it was also right, and when it wasn't I could say why. I'm not convinced you can produce AI gourmet without that. You can produce something that works, for a while, on your machine. But you can't review what you can't read, and we've just established that the review is where the quality comes from.

It's also been genuinely fun. A lot of octomon I wrote myself, by hand, as a way of learning the language properly. In fact, I let AI mostly handle the UX as it was easy to verify the output (I just look at it) and I wanted to focus on the core monitoring code. But because I didn't know Rust, I would ask it to draft a function and teach me parts of Rust that I might not have known in that context. It really was pair programming at its best and I enjoyed that as much as any of it. AI didn't replace the craft. It gave me a tireless tutor while I picked up a new one. Have I mastered Rust? Not really, but I know enough to contribute my own code and review what it's writing.

An idea that fills a real gap #

The last ingredient is the one no amount of prompting can supply. The idea has to be good. octomon exists because when my Internet connection wobbles I end up with four terminals open, ping and traceroute and dig and btop, and nine numbers that between them never answer the question I actually have. You can find eight or ten tools that each do a piece of this. I could not find one that put the whole connection on one screen and told me what was going on. I had spent years looking for a good tool and never found what I wanted. So I built one. If the thing you're building already exists in five better forms, speed of production doesn't help you; you've just made duplicative slop.

Judge the dish #

I'll end where the food processor started. In a few years, "written with AI" will be about as remarkable a disclosure as "chopped in a food processor", and the cooks who swore by the knife will have quietly bought one. What will still matter is whether the dish is any good.

So judge the dish, not the kitchen. octomon is open source, the algorithms and thresholds are written up in tedious detail, everything it stores is listed, and it runs unprivileged so you can try it without trusting it. If you taste it and it's slop, tell me precisely what's off. That's a bug report, and I'll iterate on it. Which, if you've been paying attention, is rather the point.

Do I think octomon is AI gourmet? Nope. But it's on the stove, bubbling away and it's smelling pretty darn good. Maybe a little more salt?
